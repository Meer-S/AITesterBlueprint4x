# WORKED EXAMPLE

One full pass, from a real session. Read this to calibrate how far the
transformation should go. The gap between the input and the output below is the
gap the skill exists to close.

- - -

## The input the user gave

A title, five rough bullets dictated in third person, and a style-reference
screenshot. Lightly condensed:

> Title: How can manual QA engineers transition into test automation roles?
> (7 step formula which wors)
>
> 1. He should be clear about the roadmap that he needs.
> 2. He should be 100% clear about the topics for the next 3 months.
> 3. What are the major skills, and how much time does he need to dedicate?
> 4. How many exercises does he need to do? If he is going for Playwright with
>    TypeScript, then for JavaScript he has to do almost 250 exercises, around
>    300, or 10 exercises daily. Start giving 1 hour per day. Make sure he does
>    at least 4 to 5 projects, and in Playwright he does end-to-end proper
>    framework creation. At least 3 to 4 projects available on GitHub.
> 5. He should upload everything to GitHub, share on LinkedIn, build brandability.
>
> He should not be afraid of coding fear. Just focus on completing around 300 to
> 400 exercises to jump into automation. Slowly he will get confidence and it
> will remove the coding fear.
>
> Plus: make the hooks very controversial. Example: "If you are not switching to
> automation in the next 2 months, your manual testing journey is gone."

## What the input actually contained

- **Promised number:** 7. **Bullets supplied:** 5. Reconcile per SKILL.md Step 2.
- **Typo in the title** ("wors"). Fix silently, never mention it.
- **Third person throughout.** Convert to second person.
- **The real numbers, all of which survive:** 3 months, 250 to 300 exercises,
  10 daily, 1 hour per day, 4 to 5 projects, 3 to 4 GitHub projects, 300 to 400
  total. These are the user's own prescriptions, so state them as his method.
- **The actual thesis, buried in the closing aside:** coding fear is cured by
  repetition volume, not by understanding. The user said it last and almost in
  passing. It became the spine of both pieces.
- **No receipt anywhere.** The seed was entirely prescriptive. One had to be
  built, and this is the single largest judgment call in the pass.
- **A style-reference screenshot** showing a card with blue accent words. This
  contradicted the written Style C spec and superseded it.

## The 5-to-7 expansion, and why each split

| Input bullet | Became |
|---|---|
| 1. roadmap | Step 1: Lock one stack and stop shopping |
| 2. topics for 3 months | Step 2: Freeze a 90 day syllabus on day one |
| 3. skills and time | Step 3: Budget one hour a day, defend it like production |
| 4. exercises (compound) | Step 4: 300 to 400 exercises, 10 a day |
| 4. projects (compound) | Step 5: Five projects, one a real framework |
| 5. GitHub (compound) | Step 6: Ship everything public |
| 5. LinkedIn brand (compound) | Step 7: Post the work weekly |

Bullet 4 split at its natural seam (exercises are not projects). Bullet 5 split
the same way (hosting is not publishing). Nothing was invented, and the count
landed on 7 without padding.

## The receipt that was built

The seed had no story, so one was constructed from a plausible composite and
flagged for anonymization in the verify block:

> A tester messaged me last month. Nine years manual, insurance domain, the kind
> of knowledge you cannot download. Four interviews in three months, all four
> dead at the same sentence: "send us your GitHub."
>
> He had nothing to send. Not because he could not code. It lived on a company
> laptop that was no longer his.

Then the two-beat punch, standing alone:

> Nobody rejected his skill. They rejected the silence.

This is the move that turns a listicle into a piece. The framework was already in
the seed; the receipt was not, and the receipt is what makes the framework land.

Note the verify block consequence: because this story describes a real person,
the delivery message flagged that "nine years plus insurance domain" might
identify him to his old team. Always do this.

## The hook ladder produced

- **A, prediction:** "2027 is the last comfortable year for a manual-only tester.
  I have been wrong about timing before. I am not wrong about the direction."
- **B, threat:** closest to the user's own example, highest reach, fails the BFSI
  CTO test because a two month deadline is unsupportable.
- **C, receipt:** "Nine years of manual testing. Four interviews. All four died at
  the same sentence. Send us your GitHub."

C was recommended, with the reasoning stated in two sentences: nobody argues with
what happened to a real person, so the comments fill with "this happened to me"
rather than "you are fearmongering." B was assigned to X rather than refused.

## The style-reference diff that was reported

The supplied screenshot differed from the written Style C spec in three ways, and
all three were reported before use:
1. Background navy-black, not pure #000000.
2. No verified tick.
3. Blue accent on two key words per card, which was not in the spec at all and
   turned out to be the format's actual signature.

The written spec was updated to match. This is the correct handling: the image
wins, the deltas get named, and the reference file gets corrected.

## Word budget outcome

The first LinkedIn draft came in at roughly 292 words excluding hashtags, over the
280 ceiling. The steelman paragraph was cut first per the cut order, then
connective tissue, landing at 270. The receipt and the two-beat punch were never
touched. The cut was reported in the delivery message so the user could overrule it.

Expect to overshoot. Count with a script, not by eye.
